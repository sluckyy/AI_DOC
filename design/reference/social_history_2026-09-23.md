# Reference: Evidence Base for the Comprehensive Social History

Source: the product owner's Claude Doc, https://claude.ai/artifact/DAJqg76NvUpvacf5NUc4ca (as of 2026-09-23). Condensed extract written from a full read; the live doc is the source of truth.

## Summary

Three claims usually run together have different strengths. Association (social circumstances predict morbidity, mortality and utilisation): strong. Decision-relevance (eliciting context changes the plan and improves outcomes): moderate, chiefly Weiner's covert standardised-patient and audio-recording work. Screening effectiveness (systematic social-risk screening with referral improves health): weak and mixed, with no validated instrument. The defensible case for a comprehensive social history is contextual: it improves the fit of the care plan, and its failure mode is largely invisible in the record.

## Diagnostic contribution

The "80% from history" studies never isolate the social component. Where social history carries diagnostic weight, notably occupational history, it is the neglected component and poorly captured in records.

## Contextual relevance (Weiner and colleagues)

Covert standardised-patient study, 399 visits, 111 physicians: correct care plans 73% for uncomplicated cases, 38% with a medical complication, 22% with a contextual complication, 9% with both. Physicians probed contextual clues less readily than biomedical ones. In 774 real patients with concealed recorders, outcomes improved in 71% where care was contextualised against 46% where not. Contextual errors were more costly than medical ones, and only about 5% of the cost was discernible from record review. A randomised educational intervention lifted successful contextual planning in students from 22% to 69%. The chart-invisibility finding means the failure cannot be detected by auditing documentation.

## Domains worth capturing

The Accountable Health Communities selection rule (high-quality evidence of harm, addressable by community services, not already addressed by health care) gives five core domains in ten questions: housing instability, food insecurity (Hunger Vital Sign, two items, the only validated brief screen), transport difficulty, utility insecurity, interpersonal safety (USPSTF grade B for intimate partner violence screening in women of reproductive age; insufficient for elder abuse). Additional domains with strong outcome evidence but awkward in a screening frame: social connection and isolation (Holt-Lunstad: mortality odds raised by a quarter to a third), occupation and exposures, health literacy (better addressed by universal precautions), financial strain (benefits-cliff harm), alcohol, tobacco and other drugs (alcohol screening and brief intervention is USPSTF grade B; AUDIT-C, ASSIST), caring responsibilities (determines discharge feasibility; capture in free text), justice contact, discrimination, education, digital access (capture selectively where a service response exists).

Pragmatic rule: capture a determinant systematically when something can be done about it locally, and narratively when its value lies in shaping the care plan.

## Australian context

Almost all screen-and-intervene evidence is American, generated where uninsurance dominates; universal coverage removes a large driver, so US effect sizes should not be read across. The domain that matters most for a regional network is remoteness: AIHW mortality rate ratios rise from 1.0 in major cities to 1.5 in very remote areas; burden of disease from 174 to 244 DALY per 1,000; GP attendances fall from 6.3 to 3.3 per person; life expectancy outside capitals is 0.5 to 8.6 years lower; cancer screening participation in very remote areas is 25 to 36% against 44 to 55% regionally. For a regional South Australian network: distance is the social determinant (transport, accommodation for family, cost of returning for follow-up); the MBS 715 annual health assessment for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people already embeds social and cultural assessment, with uptake the problem; RACGP Red Book guidance is preventive-activity framed, so a local instrument should map onto it rather than import a US tool.

## Screening and intervening

Process and referral outcomes consistently positive; clinical and cost outcomes not. USPSTF technical brief (Eder 2021, only 18% RCTs): four studies tested physiologic improvement, mixed, and in two the child-health effect was not mediated by reduced social needs. Yan 2022 (28 studies, 14 RCTs): some blood pressure and smoking gains, null for HbA1c and BMI. Gottlieb 2016 RCT (1,809 caregivers): in-person navigation beat written information on self-reported needs and child health. CMS Accountable Health Communities: expenditure fell 3 to 4% with fewer ED visits, but navigation did not increase community service use and only 40% of needs were resolved, so the benefit did not travel through its own causal pathway. Intervention intensity, not screening, carries whatever effect exists.

## Measurement validity

Henrikson's review of 18 social-risk instruments found psychometric scores from -1 to 9 on a scale to 36; none reported discriminant, known-groups or structural validity, or responsiveness. Consequences: trials measure with unvalidated rulers so null findings are uninterpretable; screening-rate quality measures measure documentation; ground truth for computational work is unreliable. The BJGP Open 2025 systematic review and Delphi study is the natural starting point for a properly developed instrument.

## Harms, acceptability, burden

Harms thin rather than absent; the benefits-cliff paradox is the principal identified harm. Most patients accept social-risk screening with a meaningful minority uncomfortable; acceptability depends on whether the service can respond, and asking repeatedly while doing nothing degrades disclosure. Burden is underexamined.

## Implications

For practice: teach and expect contextual probing, not exhaustive checklists. For service design: screen systematically only where a local, reachable response exists; in a regional network transport, accommodation and carer availability pass that test where generic domains do not. For AI evaluation: documented social history is a poor proxy for elicited social history; there is no validated instrument to serve as ground truth; the interesting target is contextual probing (was the clue pursued, was the plan feasible), closer to Weiner's 4C coding than to entity extraction. Open question: whether contextual probing can be reliably identified from transcript and evaluated at scale.
